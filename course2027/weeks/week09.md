# Week 9: The human role (seminar)

**No notebook this week:** reading and discussion · [Course home](../README.md)

## Why this week matters

Next week you'll let an AI agent answer questions from your data. Before you do, decide which calls belong to a person. Suppose an agent tells a hospital's leadership that its heart-failure readmissions are "worse than peers", but it compared a 07/2021–06/2024 HRRP figure with peers' 07/2023–06/2025 figures. Someone has to catch that before the slide goes to the board. Who is that person, and what do they actually check?

## The idea

**Automation bias** is the tendency to over-rely on automated advice: accepting it when it's wrong, or not looking for what it missed. This week's reading, a systematic review of decision-support studies with a focus on healthcare, found it is a recognized effect, and that training and **emphasizing user accountability** are among the things that reduce it. In this course, accountability has a concrete form: the human sign-off on every deliverable. This week you decide what it should mean for your project.

## In class (seminar, no build)

- Show and explain: two or three midterm apps revisited. What would you change now?
- Discussion in three rounds. Come with a short written answer to each prompt.

**Discussion prompts**

1. **The hand-off.** Look at your own project's steps: choose sources, join, handle suppression, chart, explain. For each, could an AI do it unsupervised tomorrow? Which ones would you never hand over, and why? Is your reason about accuracy, accountability, or values?
2. **The rubber stamp.** Your weekly sign-off has a line for "what I'm trusting the AI on". When does a sign-off turn into a rubber stamp? What would make yours a real check rather than a formality?
3. **Who signs.** In a real hospital or health department, who should sign off on a number before it reaches a decision-maker: the analyst, a clinician, a privacy officer, a manager? What does each one know that the others don't?
4. **Speed vs. checking.** The reading names time pressure and workload as conditions that make over-reliance more likely. Where in a real analytics job would that pressure show up, and what would you protect from it?
5. **The PHI line.** Your course data is public and aggregate. If your project moved to real patient data at work, which steps would change hands, and who would have to approve them?

## Your one required deliverable

**One page** using the [human sign-off template](../templates/human_signoff.md), position section:

- **What in my project should never be automated**: name one to three specific steps and give your reason for each.
- **Who signs off**: for each step, the role that should approve it and what that person checks.
- **One thing I changed my mind about** in the discussion, or why I didn't.

## Steps

1. Do the reading (about 25 minutes).
2. Write short answers to the five prompts before class.
3. After the discussion, write your one page. You may use your AI tool to challenge your draft:
   > Here is my one-page position on what should never be automated in my healthcare data project: [paste]. Argue the opposite side as strongly as you can. Don't rewrite my page.
4. Revise it. AI can help with the drafting, but the positions and the reasons must be yours. You'll defend them in discussion.

## The check to run before you submit

Is each "never automate" item **specific to your project**, a step you could point to in your notebook, rather than a general statement about AI?
